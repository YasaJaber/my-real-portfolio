If you've opened a design-forward website in the last 18 months, you've seen them:
big, soft, *almost-cinematic* color clouds that drift slowly behind the content.
Mesh gradients are the new flat design — and there's a real reason they took over.

## They feel alive without being loud

Animation on the web is a balancing act. Too little and the page feels static.
Too much and your user gets motion sick. Mesh gradients sit at exactly the right
spot: they're *kinetic but quiet*. Your eye registers movement, your brain reads
it as ambient — and your content stays the hero.

## They photograph well

That sounds weird, but hear me out: in an era where every screenshot ends up on
Twitter, X, Threads, or a designer's mood board, your hero needs to look great
**as a still image** too. Mesh gradients give you painterly depth in a single
frame.

## They're cheap

A well-built mesh background is just:

```css
background:
  radial-gradient(at 20% 0%, #A855F7 0%, transparent 50%),
  radial-gradient(at 80% 100%, #06B6D4 0%, transparent 50%),
  #030303;
```

That's it. No PNG, no JPG, no Lottie. Pixel-perfect at any resolution.

> The performance budget cost of a beautiful background dropped to almost zero.

## Where it gets interesting

The next step — the one I'm playing with on this site — is **interactive**
mesh: blobs that drift with the cursor, that ripple on click, that shift hue as
you scroll. Built right (Canvas 2D, not 100 stacked divs) it stays under 1ms per
frame on a mid-range phone.

If you want to peek at the implementation, scroll back to the homepage and
**click anywhere**. The colors should subtly shift each time. That's intentional.
